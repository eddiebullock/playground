# Focus groups 2-4: what they mean for Cue

Read on 2026-10-25 from the Teams transcripts (session 2: 14 Sep 2026;
session 3: 5 Oct; session 4: 19 Oct). Session 1 was an introduction and has
no transcript in the folder. Four to six autistic adults took part in each
session. One participant contributed by chat only, and Eddie read those
messages aloud.

**Privacy.** Participants are coded P1-P6. This file paraphrases and never
quotes, and it holds no names. Each point cites a session and a timestamp
(for example S2 1:16), so it can be checked against the transcript on
OneDrive. Verbatim quotes stay in the transcripts, which never leave this
machine. Codes: P1 is the participant who raised speed and dependence; P2
raised local processing and acknowledge-and-dismiss; P3 is the older
participant who raised verification and oversharing; P4 needs processing
time and wants settings; P5 raised white lies, invitations and distraction;
P6 is the chat-only participant.

These are themes from one small group (n = 6). They are design input, not
findings that generalise.

---

## 1. Themes

### T1. Trust means accuracy, speed, local processing and outside endorsement
- Trust needs the tool to be right *and* fast. In a live conversation, ten
  seconds is too long (P1, S2 1:16-2:01; summarised and agreed, S4 2:34-3:28).
- Free AI tools are often wrong, so the group starts out wary (P1, S4 3:10-3:28).
- Keeping everything on the device makes it easier to trust. A cloud tool
  that builds up a picture of your relationships would be much harder to
  accept (P2, S2 2:04-2:49).
- Trust would come from testing and endorsement by an organisation they
  already trust, such as the National Autistic Society (P3, S2 4:37-5:48;
  again S4 4:03).
- Some participants doubt that any tool can be "correct" when people are
  being deceptive, so subjectivity has to be acknowledged (P5, S4 2:34).

### T2. Not a lie detector: read what most people would take, kindly
- Social white lies (for example "your haircut looks great") are often kind.
  A tool that flags them as untrue could do harm (P5, S2 6:54-8:20).
- The ethical target is what a typical person would conclude from the
  situation, not lie detection (P2, chat, read at S2 12:58).
- The aim should be what helps the interaction for everyone, not only the
  user (P3, S2 9:09-11:21).
- On the other side, several participants want to know whether an offer is
  *genuine*. The example was a new acquaintance saying "we should have
  coffee sometime", and whether that was a real invitation or a pleasantry
  (P5, S3 2:00-11:56).

### T3. The user decides; the tool offers perspectives
- The group wants control, and wants suggestions they can take or leave. The
  tool should never decide what is right (summarised and agreed, S4 16:39-18:20).
- Readings should be framed as perspectives to consider, not prescriptions
  (P6, S3 37:11; S4 23:41).
- A choose-your-own-adventure framing: options, not one answer, and
  nothing done on autopilot (P5, S3 33:26).
- There should be a way to correct the tool when it is wrong, using what
  the user knows about the situation (P2, chat, S2 12:19).
- They want to tell the tool their own view, and to ask it why it is
  suggesting something (P6, S4 23:41).
- What counts as awkward is individual. Prompts must be easy to acknowledge
  and dismiss so they do not nag (P2, S2 33:34-34:15).

### T4. The tool is for me, and sometimes it is for them
- One participant could not tell whether the tool would help them understand
  situations or help them seem neurotypical, and asked why autistic people
  should be the ones to adapt (P1, S2 36:44-38:14). The group agreed.
- Non-autistic people should use AI to understand that autistic behaviour is
  different, not wrong (P5 and P6, chat, read at S3 25:10).
- Disclosure can help: when someone knows what you use, they adapt (P1,
  S4 8:54).

### T5. Learning, dependence and choice
- One participant fears dependence, based on watching a colleague become
  unable to work without AI. They want something that helps them learn, not
  something that does it for them (P1, S2 40:59-42:25; S4 18:24-19:57).
- Another does not want to be expected to learn eye contact. They are happy to
  use an aid, as they do with a walking stick (P4, read at S2 44:31).
- The resolution the group reached was **choice**: support or learning, as
  the user prefers (P1, S2 45:13-45:29). The tool should give information
  about the *environment and situation*, not about the user's own behaviour
  (P2, S2 45:56).
- An assistant that the user configures, with ground rules agreed up front
  (P3, S4 26:44-27:23). A dashboard with settings that learns from the user
  (P4, S2 12:39; S4 27:38).

### T6. Context and memory about people
- Advice that knows only the immediate moment is a pitfall. Trust grows if the
  tool knows details about the people involved and the history between them
  (P6, read at S2 0:03 and S4 6:00).
- But other people may not know they are being profiled, which raises
  fairness and consent questions (P1, S2 2:56-3:39).

### T7. After the interaction is where much of the work happens
- Participants process after the event, not in the moment. That is why a
  reflective tool would help (P4, S2 6:16-6:38; P5, S4 32:28-32:57: the
  replay on the drive home).
- Making sense of a conflict afterwards: was it me or them? (P3, S3
  4:57-9:26: an acquaintance reacted angrily, and a witness later said the
  reaction was unnecessary.) Readings of events should allow that the other
  person's reaction may be about them.
- Rumination over expected patterns that were not met (P6, S3 54:19-55:02).

### T8. Options with consequences, and what was left unsaid
- Two or three options for what to do or say next, each with its possible
  consequences, so the user can see what may happen before acting (P6, S3
  27:00-28:36).
- Account for what was *not* said: unspoken understandings are tiring (P6,
  S3 34:39; S4 6:00).
- Get to the nub of a long or vague message (P3, S3 36:45-37:11). A real
  example was a surgery reschedule, where "when suits you?" was ambiguous
  between before or after the original date (P3, S3 34:57-35:46).

### T9. Validity and reassurance, never suppression
- Reassure that the user's way of interacting is valid (P6, S3 24:03-24:42).
- Strong objection to anything like a prompt that says "stop talking about
  your interest". Hearing someone talk about their passion is something to
  value (P5, S3 19:02-20:02; S4 25:22-25:59).
- One participant would *choose* a prompt about oversharing and turn-taking
  for themselves (P3, S2 50:32; S3 14:41; S4 26:44, 48:59). Another
  framed it as reassurance plus an optional reminder to take turns (P6, S3
  24:03). So this must be opt-in, configured by the user, and never a default.
- A sense of completion: something that says "you have explained enough,
  it's done" to stop going over things (P3, S2 50:32).

### T10. Safety and boundaries
- Help to notice when someone may not have good intentions, without making
  the user any less kind (P6, S3 45:47-46:45; S4 29:27).
- Real example: a neighbour who would not accept "no" to an invitation (P3,
  S3 47:41-48:45).
- Caution from P3 in S2 (10:07-10:32): AI may not understand nuance well
  enough to warn of danger, and the tool should not assume bad intent.
  Resolution: affirm boundaries ("you are allowed to say no") rather than
  label people as dangerous.

### T11. Form factor: glasses are disliked; phone, earpiece and haptics preferred
- Personal discomfort with glasses. A watch or earbuds would be preferred
  (P5, S4 8:17; P6, S4 15:37).
- Media stigma about smart glasses: "pervert glasses", MPs wearing them, and
  the charge that an aid means you are not really competent (S2 24:09; P4,
  S4 14:21-15:19).
- Workplace rules may forbid recording (P2, S2 16:47-17:50).
- Live text or a voice in the ear is distracting and makes it easy to lose the
  thread. A small vibration that says "pause and think" would be better (P5,
  S4 31:11-32:57).
- Too much information is already a problem (P1, S4 33:44).
- "Can it just be a phone app?" (P1, S4 45:04). An app with settings, plus a
  ring with a button and a vibration (P4, S4 46:02-46:42).
- Varifocal glasses wearers cannot use smart glasses easily (P1, S4 9:33).
- Talking to the AI out loud is not possible with others present, and typing
  looks rude (P6, S4 42:42).
- Inward sensing for self-care (anxiety, fatigue, energy accounting) was
  suggested independently (P6, S4 42:42). This matches the Emteq fit in
  PLAN section 2.

## 2. What this changes in Cue

| Theme | Decision | Status |
|---|---|---|
| T1 | Keep local-only. Show the evidence (accuracy, calibration, abstention) on an "How good is it?" panel. Seek NAS or advisory-group endorsement before Study 5 launch. | Panel: build. Endorsement: ask Eddie. |
| T1 | Speed: Reflect has no live latency need, but should still be fast. Measure end-to-end time on the M3 (E4). | Measure. |
| T2 | Readings describe what most people would take from the words, framed kindly. Never "they are lying". A white lie is read as kindness. | Generation prompt rule: add. |
| T2 | Add a "genuine offer or pleasantry?" case to the development set. | Add. |
| T3 | "None of these" / "My reading is..." correction. The user's own reading is saved alongside Cue's, and Cue scores it alongside the others. | Build. |
| T3 | "Why?" is already shown as cues. Keep the cues visible. | Done. |
| T5 | **Your read first** (optional): the user writes or picks their own reading before seeing Cue's. It supports learning, autonomy and confidence, and gives Study 5 an outcome (agreement between user and Cue over time). Off by default, as a choice. | Build. |
| T6 | **People notes**: optional, short notes the user writes about a person ("always brief on Slack"), stored only on this computer and added as context. No automatic profiling of other people. | Build. |
| T7 | "What happened" input: accept a short description of an event, not only a quoted line, and allow readings where the reaction is about the other person. | Prompt: extend. |
| T8 | **Ways to reply** (opt-in, after the readings): 2-3 options, including "wait or ask", each with how it might land. The user's own words and style are kept. No masking scripts. | Build. |
| T9 | Guardrails: never advise the user to talk less about their interests, mask, or change how they come across. Add to the E4 audit. | Prompt and audit: add. |
| T9 | Two-way mode: when the draft already says what the user means, say so plainly ("this already says it"). | Prompt: add. |
| T10 | Boundary affirmation: when a message pressures the user after a "no", include a reading that names the pressure, and remind the user they can say no. Do not label the person. | Prompt: add, evaluate on dev cases. |
| T4 | Two-way is a core mode, not an extra. Universal-design wording. A future "reading an autistic colleague" mode for non-autistic users. | Record in PLAN. |
| T11 | Live mode: haptic or very short cues by default, and an earpiece option. Smart-glasses camera work deprioritised further. Phone first (PWA). | Update `live_and_wearable.md`. |

## 3. Questions for session 5 (Eddie's next session, on where it would and would not help)

1. Would you use "your read first", or would it feel like a test?
2. People notes: comfortable, or uneasy about notes on others even if private?
3. Ways to reply: helpful, or too close to scripting?
4. Is "most people would take it as..." the right framing, given T2?
5. Would a link or card that explains the tool to others help (T4, S2 18:43)?
