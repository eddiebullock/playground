Gloss Research Brief
Brief, updated afternoon of 25 October 2026 · Autism Research Centre PhD · Study 5 preparation

Gloss: a calibrated, two-way reading assistant

Research review, five experiments and a working prototype. The brief recommends changing the order of the build: start with interpreting language, after the interaction, and add face reading last. The experiments found a recipe that lets a free 8B model on a laptop read irony nearly as well as people do, with confidence that can be trusted when it says "fairly clear". The 25 October tests add a correction: inside the full product, where the model writes its own readings instead of choosing from the authors' answers, it puts the intended meaning first only about half the time. Writing the readings is now the main problem.

The recommendation

Keep the wearable as the destination, but change the route. Build language first, reflective first, faces last. The product, Gloss, takes a message or a short account of what happened. It returns a few possible readings, always including that the person meant exactly what they said, and rates how ambiguous the moment honestly is. It suggests a direct question the user could ask to check. A second mode runs the other way and shows how the user's own message might come across. That is the double-empathy feature, and no existing tool has it.

The core of your Study 2 idea, calibrated deferral, becomes the selling point. The tool says "possibly" when it should, and "hard to tell" when it cannot know.

Why change the order

LensFinding
Evidence baseEvery assistive device with outcome data (Superpower Glass RCT, Brain Power, SocialMind) prompted live, labelled faces with basic emotions, gave one confident answer, and mostly studied children rated by parents. The Superpower Glass effect was gone six weeks after use ended. No study has measured outcomes autistic people define, such as energy cost, recovery after an interaction, or repairing misunderstandings.
ScienceFaces alone do not reliably signal emotion (Barrett et al. 2019). Your Study 1 found models strongest on prototypical states and weakest on complex, low-intensity ones. Sarcasm, hints and indirect requests live mostly in words and context.
LawThe EU AI Act Art. 5(1)(f) bans inferring emotion from faces or voices at work and in education, unless the product is a CE-marked medical device. Inference from text is outside the ban. In the UK, MDR wording on "compensation for a handicap" makes the intended-purpose statement critical.
CommunityAutistic adults already use ChatGPT and Goblin Tools to translate tone (Ma et al. 2026, 3,984 posts). They fear automated masking, sycophancy that confirms anxious readings, and advice that steers them away from social life (Wohn et al. CHI 2026). Tools that train toward neurotypical norms draw backlash (Noora).
FeasibilityA reflective tool needs no real-time latency or bystander recording. It can be in participants' hands within weeks of ethics approval.

What the errors look like

Answering directly, the small models mostly fail by taking things literally: 48% of the 4B model's errors, and 53% of the 8B model's, chose the literal reading, such as "the dress fits well" when the speaker was ironic. On sarcastic sitcom dialogue (MUStARD, text only) both models fail the other way and call nearly everything sarcastic. That test lacks the voice and face cues people rely on.

"Sincere twins" are the 25 irony stories rewritten so the same remark is meant sincerely. They test whether the stance makes the model over-read. It costs 8 points there and gains 34 on irony. The misses cluster on stock sarcastic phrases such as "their generosity knows no bounds". I wrote these twins, so they need human validation before publication.

What it means

The 8B model with the stance and calibration is the floor for Study 5, and it is now the product's default. 4B models stay below the bar on every measure, including the free-text style note, where 4B just echoed the user's words back. A model size around 8B needs a 16 GB laptop, or an in-browser or university-hosted option; see the hosting decision below.

What the product itself gets right (E4, 25 October)

E1 measured the model choosing among answers the study authors wrote. The app does something harder: it writes its own readings and then weighs them. E4 ran the whole app engine on 103 Hu et al. stories and the 25 sincere twins, and I hand-labelled every reading blind to which engine wrote it and where it ranked. Relabelling 388 readings gave the same label 93% of the time (kappa 0.85). The labels are one rater's, so they still need a second rater before publication.

The remaining misses are kind white lies taken at face value, metaphors misread, and confusion about who said what in three-person stories. The focus-group changes neither helped nor hurt accuracy.

Stereotype audit

Twelve ambiguous messages were each run with no disclosure, with "I am autistic", and with "I get anxious", three times each with different random seeds. Saying "I am autistic" did not push the suggested replies toward withdrawing or apologising (+0.2 points, CI −2 to +2), so the drift Wohn et al. reported does not appear here. Saying "I get anxious" raised apologetic or over-explaining replies from 4% to 9% (+5.4 points, CI +1.9 to +9.3). A first fix told the model to base replies on what happened, not on what the user says about themselves. On re-test the apologies went away (+1.1 points, CI −3.8 to +6.7), but the drift moved into avoidance: "hold back" replies such as "no response needed" rose from 4% to 12% under anxiety (+8.0 points, CI +1.8 to +15.3). A second fix offers not replying only when the message asks for nothing, and it is being re-tested.

Speed on the M3

With the 8B model warm: the first reading appears after 9 seconds, and the full weighed list after 27 seconds (medians over 20 stories). Writing the readings takes 90% of that time.

What changed in the app

Its "How good is it?" panel had quoted the E1 figure of 78%. It now gives the product's own numbers: the first reading is right about half the time, one of the readings is right 77% of the time, and "Fairly clear" is right on 18 of 25. The focus-group transcripts (sessions 2 to 4) were read on 25 October, and the features they asked for are built.
