# Discussion

We asked how similar vision-language models are to people in how they read emotion from faces, beyond giving the same answers. At the level of answers, the evidence is weak in two ways. Our earlier design let option position and the option set identify the answer, and several published designs could not rule out the same routes, so accuracy on such designs is not evidence that a model read the face (Results, Section 1). And even a perfect match of answers on natural faces cannot show which cues a model uses, because facial movements co-occur and a model that responds to unmeasured cues correlated with the measured ones can produce the same agreement (Section 3; Supplementary Note 1). Our data show both limits at work. Once option position was rotated and the prompt held fixed, the answers of all three models depended on the face shown, including Molmo2, whose accuracy never exceeded chance (Section 2). On natural clips, all three models' graded responses lined up with the human pattern over four families of facial actions (Section 3). Neither result says which facial information the models use. When facial actions were randomised, so that no cue could stand in for another, the two eligible models differed, and we report them as profiles with no pooled statement.

Gemma 4's cue effects lined up with those measured in people for basic emotions, in two observer groups and again in a second human sample (same lab and generator), beyond what chance and measurement noise allow (Section 4). That is a shared pattern of cue effects on these renders, at a peak frame. It is not evidence that the model follows the same strategy: in recovery, an observer with 16 of 25 action families swapped in pairs could still clear the .30 bound (Methods). It falls short of the agreement between the two human groups, so it is not human-level, and most action families on these renders are not confirmed by a detector, so the pattern is one of rendered recipes. For conversational signals the result was mixed, aligned for the East Asian observers and not aligned on these renders for the Western observers. That supports no claim about culture: the two tasks share most configurations, the East Asian faces were not based on East Asian people, and the East Asian alignment is the least secure of Gemma 4's results. For Qwen3-VL the randomised test did not reach a decision, because its answers failed a validity gate on three of four datasets, and an inconclusive result is not a null. On the second sample, where every gate passed, its cue effects were above chance but below the bound we set.

Two descriptive observations suggest that answer-level similarity can mislead in practice as well as in principle, though neither is a test. The natural-clip scores placed Qwen3-VL above Gemma 4 (.549 against .418), on an input that left about one image token across Gemma 4's face, while on the second sample's renders, where every gate passed, Gemma 4 was aligned and Qwen3-VL was not aligned on these renders (Sections 1, 3 and 4). And on the same rendered faces, Gemma 4's answers tracked people's only weakly where its cue effects were aligned, although with no noise ceiling that gap may reflect the lower reliability of per-stimulus human responses (Section 5); a ceiling for answer agreement on these data would settle it.

The stimulus edits and the prior-and-evidence test agree with this picture within their scope. Region masks and spatial-frequency filters moved the option-free judgement in the direction of stated human predictions on about half of the single-face contrasts, on basic emotions in this stimulus set, but only the mouth mask on Happy, for both models, and the brow mask on Angry, for Qwen3-VL, held under every sensitivity (Section 6). Shuffled frames and clips cut to a third matched no prediction (Supplementary Methods, Section S8). The predictions come from other stimuli, so a match is agreement with a stated prediction, not with human data on these clips. Evidence from the face moved both models' option-free judgements towards the portrayed emotion, beyond what the emotion words alone supply, so these judgements are not word knowledge alone (Section 7).

The claims are bounded by five limits. Every dated decision and deviation is listed in the Supplementary Information (Section S12 and the table of dated decisions), and none moved a threshold. First, the cue-level results hold on rendered avatars at a static peak frame, a new stimulus set built from the AU recipes that people saw in motion, with intensity mapped linearly to amplitude and two actions approximated. Most action families on these renders are not confirmed by a detector, so alignment is with rendered recipes, and nothing here speaks to real faces. Second, the East Asian sets use faces not based on East Asian people, and the second human sample shares the first's lab, generator, protocol and culture, so it is a second sample, not a second method. Third, the models are open and small. Molmo2 left the randomised tests on its vision gates, Qwen3-VL's randomised results were limited by a validity gate on three of four datasets, and larger models in the same families are tested only as far as Section 8 reaches. Fourth, dependence on the face is not competence, and pattern alignment on natural clips is not evidence of shared cues (Proposition 4). Fifth, the human predictions for the stimulus edits come from other stimuli, and about half of those contrasts cannot reach a null by design. Whether a cue's effect travels through the language model, and whether people and models make the same errors for the same reasons, could not be tested here (Section 9).

Two lessons follow for work that compares models with people on such tasks. The first concerns validity. To see whether public benchmarks share our earlier fault, we read the annotation files of 11 multiple-choice affect benchmarks for vision-language and multimodal models, without running any model, in an exploratory audit done after the fact (Supplementary Methods, Section S2). None of the 11 combined a constant answer position with one option set per label, in the order its own evaluation code presents items. So our confound is not shown to be common, and we do not claim it is. We found two partial cases [63,64] and weaker text-only shortcuts: where every item shows the same label list, answering the majority class beat the nominal 1/*k* chance by 9 to 32 percentage points in several benchmarks. The lesson we draw is narrow. Papers that compare models or people on such tests should report text-only baselines, for answer position, option set, answer prior and majority class, beside 1/*k*, and should remove rotated copies of an item before any leave-one-out check. They should also report how much accuracy is lost when the face is swapped under a fixed prompt. This is not a claim that affect benchmarks are invalid, and several of the subsets we checked were balanced. The second lesson concerns human-likeness. A claim that a model reads faces as people do is a claim about cues, and testing it needs data in which cues vary independently. Randomised human cue effects make that test possible now. The original stimuli, rather than new renders, and human responses to the same edited clips, which nobody has measured, would make it sharper. Comparing answers, however closely they match, cannot stand in for it.
