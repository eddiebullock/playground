Vision-language models (VLMs) can take an image or video frames and answer questions about them in words. Several reports find that such models match or beat people on tests of emotion and mental-state recognition from faces, including the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test (RMET) and sets of posed expressions [1-3]. These reports invite a larger question: do models read faces the way people do? Schyns and colleagues split that question into three degrees of similarity: the same behaviour, the same stimulus features, and the same computations [4]. The VLM emotion work we located sits almost entirely at the first degree.

Two problems stand in the way of going further. A score on a multiple-choice task may not measure face reading at all. And even a clean score cannot show which facial information a system uses.

The first problem is one of validity. Language models are sensitive to the order and form of answer options. In text models, option order can shift accuracy by a wide margin [6], and selection bias has been traced mainly to the option identifiers [5]. In one VLM preprint, most predictions fell on the first slot under one scoring method [7]. Option order also moves VLM judgements of the emotion an image evokes [8]. Prompt text is a second route. If each target label comes with its own set of options, the prompt identifies the category, and a system can use that with no image at all.

Our own earlier task had both faults. The target sat in the first position on all 243 items, and each of the 21 labels had one option set. Accuracy therefore could not be told apart from a preference for the first option, so we withdrew that design before any of the work reported here (Methods).

We then asked how far published claims escape these faults. We examined eight recent papers on VLM emotion judgement, mostly through targeted passages, so this is a spot check and not a systematic review. One tested option order directly, on images rather than faces [8]. One randomised it as a routine control [9]. Two RMET studies fixed it [1,2]. One did not report it [11], one did not control it [12], and in one the question did not apply [13]. The eighth avoids option lists [10], and we did not check its baselines. In the seven we could check, we found none that controlled option position and also swapped the face under a fixed prompt. The nearest is a design that crossed neutral text with paired images [13]. So most of the VLM emotion claims we located cannot separate perception from option order and prompt text.

An audit also has to ask whether the face reaches the model, and whether the target is fair. Common input formats can leave very few image tokens across a face (Methods). Acted labels are a further problem. On items acted as Kind, Unfriendly or Jealous, face-only human observers chose the acted label on only 23 to 26% of responses, against a chance level of 17% [14]. A model that disagrees with the acted label on those items may be agreeing with people, and a model that scores far above people there may be using something other than the face.

The second problem is one of identifiability. Facial movements co-occur on natural faces, so a system that uses one cue can look like a system that uses another. Behavioural metrics such as error consistency [15] compare outputs on natural items. We show that this comparison cannot settle cue use. If two systems give the same responses on every natural item, every behavioural metric on those items gives the same value, however many trials are collected. Yet their cue effects, meaning the change in response when one cue changes and the others do not, can still take any pattern at all (Methods). For linear observers, the amount that behaviour constrains cue effects depends on the smallest eigenvalue of the natural cue covariance. These results are elementary. The construction behind the first does not show that trained networks behave this way, and limits of a related kind have been discussed elsewhere [16,17]. Their practical meaning is direct: to learn which cues a system uses, one has to break the natural correlations between cues.

Human data of that kind exist. In reverse-correlation studies, observers judge faces whose facial action units (AUs) are set at random [18,19]. Randomisation identifies the effect of each AU on human judgement. This gives a target for a sharper comparison. We call it Causal Feature Alignment (CFA): the similarity between a matrix of randomised human cue effects and the matrix of cue effects measured from a model under the same kind of design.

We located no study comparing a vision-language model's response to named facial cues with effects estimated from human observers under randomised cue presentation. Coarse image-level counterfactuals on VLMs exist [13], as do latent AU interventions on an affective model [20]. Related work edits race and gender in face images [21], tests whether facial regions are necessary for fine-tuned models [22], and relates annotated teeth visibility to VLM performance [23]. None of these, as far as we read them, compares a model with randomised human cue effects. This statement rests on searches run on 7 November 2026 of arXiv and OpenAlex and on web search. Semantic Scholar was effectively unavailable, Google Scholar and unpublished work were not searched, CCN 2026 was not covered, and VSS and CogSci 2026 were covered only in part. Most of the papers above were not read in full.

Here we ask how similar VLMs are to humans in how they read mental states from faces, not only in the answers they give. We take three steps. First, we audit validity: with option position controlled, how much of each model's accuracy disappears when the prompt stays the same and the face is swapped? Second, we test feature use, on natural clips and then on faces rendered from the AU recipes that human observers judged, comparing each model's cue effects with randomised human effects. Third, we intervene on stimuli, using region masks, spatial-frequency filters and changes to frame order, and ask whether each model's judgement moves as stated human predictions say it should.

All confirmatory tests were fixed in advance [PENDING: A4 freeze; true for the randomised cue test only once it is frozen before any model sees a render], and each is reported under a preregistered rule that can return a match, a mismatch or an inconclusive result. Causal claims in this paper are about the models. The human side is a fixed target taken from other stimuli, and we make no claim about how people would respond to an edited clip. Results concern the recognition of acted conventions in these stimuli, not the reading of felt states [24].
