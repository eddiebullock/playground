Vision-language models (VLMs) can take an image or video frames and answer questions about them in words. Several reports find that some such models match or exceed human norms on tests of emotion and mental-state recognition, including versions of the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test (RMET), which uses eye-region images [1,2], and a set of posed expressions [3]. The record is mixed: in those same reports other models fall short [2,3], and accuracy falls below human levels on inverted eye images [1]. These reports invite a larger question: do models read faces the way people do? Schyns and colleagues split that question into three embedded degrees of similarity: the same behaviour, then the same stimulus features, then the same computations on those features [4]. The VLM emotion work we located mostly compares scores, which is at most the first degree. Some of it compares no human data, and some goes below behaviour into model internals [9,10,22].

Two problems stand in the way of going further. A score on a multiple-choice task may not measure face reading at all. And even a clean score cannot show which facial information a system uses.

The first problem is one of validity. Language models are sensitive to the order and form of answer options. In two text models, the gap between the best and worst option orderings reached about 13 to 75 percentage points [6], and selection bias has been traced mainly to the option identifiers [5]. In one VLM preprint, on a science question set, Qwen2.5-VL-7B picked the first slot, always labelled A, on 94% of items when a reasoning cue was appended and the answer label was read at once, against 52% without the cue [7]. Prompt text is a second route. If each target label comes with its own set of options, the prompt identifies the category, and a system can use that with no image at all.

Our own earlier task had both faults. The target sat in the first position on all 243 items, and each of the 21 labels had one option set. Accuracy therefore could not be told apart from a preference for the first option, so we withdrew that design before any of the work reported here (Methods).

We then asked how far published claims escape these faults. We examined eight recent papers that use VLM emotion tasks [1,2,8-13], one of them a mechanistic study with a facial-expression probe [9], and coded each design (Fig. 1e). This is a spot check and not a systematic review, and some supplements stayed out of reach. Across the eight, we found none that estimated the accuracy lost when the face is swapped under a fixed prompt, with option position controlled or absent. So several of the VLM emotion claims we located cannot rule out option order or, where option sets differ by item, prompt text. Others avoid one or both faults but do not measure how much accuracy depends on the face.

The second problem is one of identifiability. Facial movements co-occur on natural faces, so a system that uses one cue can look like a system that uses another. Behavioural metrics such as error consistency [15] compare responses on a shared item set. We show that, on natural items, this comparison cannot settle cue use. If two systems give the same responses on every natural item, every behavioural metric on those items gives the same value, however many trials are collected. Yet their cue effects, meaning the change in response when one cue changes and the others do not, can still take any pattern at all (Methods). For linear observers, the amount that behaviour constrains cue effects depends on the smallest eigenvalue of the natural cue covariance. These results are elementary. The construction behind the first does not show that trained networks behave this way. The multiple-realizability argument for why behaviour falls short is made by Schyns and colleagues [4], and the comparison we define below is one way to build the second-degree comparison they call for. The practical meaning of these results is direct. Natural data can carry some information about cue use, for linear observers (Methods) and for tests of a model's own concepts [17], but nothing guarantees it: a system can differ off the natural items, respond to cues that were not measured, or be nonlinear. To learn which cues a system uses without those assumptions, one has to break the natural correlations between cues.

Human data of that kind exist. In reverse-correlation studies, observers judge faces whose facial action units (AUs) are set at random [19,42]. Randomisation, within the generator's morphological constraints, identifies the effect of each AU on human judgement. This gives a target for a sharper comparison. We call it Cue Effect Alignment (CEA): the similarity between a matrix of randomised human cue effects and the matrix of cue effects measured from a model under the same kind of design. Comparing judges by their cue weights is long established in judgement research, in the lens model and policy capturing [65,66], and a recent study compares language models with organisations by the cosine of regression weights fitted to observed cases [62]. CEA differs from the latter in using effects of randomised cues rather than weights fitted to natural cases, for the reasons above (Supplementary Note 1, Section 6.4).

We located no study comparing a vision-language model's response to named facial cues with effects estimated from human observers under randomised cue presentation. Coarse image-level counterfactuals on VLMs exist [13], as do latent AU interventions on an affective model [20]. None of these, as far as we read them, compares a model with randomised human cue effects. The searches behind this statement, and what they did not cover, are given in Supplementary Methods, Section S2.

Here we ask how similar VLMs are to people in how they read faces, at the level of cues as well as answers. We measure how much of each model's accuracy is lost when the face is swapped under a fixed prompt with option position rotated; we compare its cue effects with randomised human effects, on faces rendered from the AU recipes people judged, in two human samples from the same laboratory and face generator [60]; and we test whether masking, filtering and reordering the stimuli move its judgement as human studies predict. All three models' answers depended on the face, and all three aligned with the human response pattern on natural clips, which cannot show shared cue use. With cues randomised, one model's cue effects aligned with people's for basic emotions, though less closely than two human groups agree with each other, and the other model's were not shown to align.

Each confirmatory test reported here was fixed in a dated amendment or decision-log entry before its data were analysed, and Methods lists every deviation with its date. The rules for the randomised cue test, the stimulus interventions and the prior-and-evidence test can each return a match, a mismatch or an inconclusive result (Methods). Causal claims in this paper are about the models, and we make no claim about the route a cue takes through a model, because the literal test of that route cannot fail (Methods). The human side is a fixed target taken from other stimuli, and we make no claim about how people would respond to an edited clip. Results concern agreement with acted or rendered expression conventions in these stimuli, not the reading of felt states [24].
