Some vision-language models are reported to match people at reading mental states from faces. We ask how similar these models are to humans in how they read faces, not only in the answers they give. The manuscript makes three contributions.

First, a validity audit. Our own earlier task placed the correct option first on all 243 items and gave each of 21 labels one option set, so its accuracy could not be told apart from position or prompt text. We withdrew it. In a spot check of eight recent papers, several of the claims we located cannot rule out option order or prompt text. We then measured how much accuracy each model loses when the face is swapped under a fixed prompt, with option position rotated. [PENDING: one sentence on RQ0, once A1 is locked.]

Second, an identifiability result. We prove that agreement on natural items cannot settle which facial cues a system uses, because natural cues are correlated. We define Cue Effect Alignment, a comparison of a model's cue effects with human effects estimated under randomised cue presentation.

Third, a cue-level test. We compare model cue effects on rendered faces with randomised human cue effects from public data, against a noise ceiling and a between-group reference.
